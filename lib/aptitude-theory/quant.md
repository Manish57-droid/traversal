=== Percentages
---
**Per cent** means "per hundred". So *x%* is simply the fraction *x/100*. Almost every percentage question becomes easy once you turn percentages into **fractions** or **multipliers**.

### Core formulas

| What you want | Formula |
|---|---|
| x% of N | (x / 100) × N |
| A is what % of B | (A / B) × 100 |
| % change | (Change / Original value) × 100 |
| Increase N by x% | N × (1 + x/100) |
| Decrease N by x% | N × (1 − x/100) |

> **Always divide by the original value.** "Increased from 80 to 100" is a 25% increase (20/80), but "decreased from 100 to 80" is only a 20% decrease (20/100).

### Shortcut 1 — Learn the fraction table

Knowing these by heart saves the most time in exams:

| % | Fraction | % | Fraction |
|---|---|---|---|
| 50% | 1/2 | 12.5% | 1/8 |
| 33.33% | 1/3 | 11.11% | 1/9 |
| 25% | 1/4 | 10% | 1/10 |
| 20% | 1/5 | 9.09% | 1/11 |
| 16.67% | 1/6 | 8.33% | 1/12 |
| 14.28% | 1/7 | 6.25% | 1/16 |

```calc
Find 37.5% of 640.
37.5% = 3/8
3/8 × 640 = 3 × 80 = 240
```

### Shortcut 2 — x% of y = y% of x

4% of 75 looks awkward, but it is the same as 75% of 4 = **3**.

### Shortcut 3 — Successive change (the a + b + ab/100 rule)

If a value changes by **a%** and then by **b%**, the net change is:

**Net % = a + b + (a × b) / 100** (use a minus sign for a decrease)

```calc
Price rises 20%, then falls 10%. Net change?
Net = 20 + (−10) + (20 × −10)/100
    = 10 − 2 = +8%  → an 8% increase
```

```calc
Increase by 25%, then decrease by 20%:
Net = 25 − 20 − (25 × 20)/100 = 5 − 5 = 0% → no change
Check with multipliers: 1.25 × 0.8 = 1.00
```

> An equal rise and fall (x% up, then x% down) **always** gives a net loss of x²/100 %. For example, 10% up then 10% down = 1% loss.

### Shortcut 4 — Price change and consumption

If the price of an item **rises by r%**, the cut in consumption needed to keep spending the same is:

**r / (100 + r) × 100 %**

If the price **falls by r%**, the rise in consumption that keeps spending the same is:

**r / (100 − r) × 100 %**

```calc
Sugar price falls 20%. Increase consumption by how much to spend the same?
20 / (100 − 20) × 100 = 20/80 × 100 = 25%
```

### Shortcut 5 — "A is more than B" vs "B is less than A"

If A is **r% more** than B, then B is **r / (100 + r) × 100 %** less than A.

```calc
A's salary is 20% more than B's. B is less than A by?
20 / 120 × 100 = 16.67%
```

### Shortcut 6 — Assume 100

When no actual number is given, assume the starting value is **100**. Every percentage then becomes a plain number.

```calc
In an election, the winner gets 60% and wins by 200 votes. Total votes?
Winner 60, loser 40 → margin = 20% of total
20% of total = 200 → total = 200 × 100/20 = 1000
```

### Population and depreciation

Growth of r% per year for n years: **P × (1 + r/100)ⁿ**
Depreciation of r% per year for n years: **P × (1 − r/100)ⁿ**

To go **backwards** (population n years ago), divide by the same factor.

```calc
Population now = 24200, growth = 10% per year. Population 2 years ago?
P × 1.1 × 1.1 = 24200 → P × 1.21 = 24200 → P = 20000
```

### Common traps
- Two successive 10% rises are **21%**, not 20%.
- "Percentage points" is not the same as "percent". Going from 10% to 12% is a rise of 2 percentage points, which is a 20% increase.
- Read carefully whether the question asks for the **new value** or the **change**.

=== Profit and Loss
---
Every profit and loss question is built on two prices:

- **Cost Price (CP)** — what the seller paid.
- **Selling Price (SP)** — what the seller received.

If SP > CP, there is a **profit**. If SP < CP, there is a **loss**.

### Core formulas

| Quantity | Formula |
|---|---|
| Profit | SP − CP |
| Loss | CP − SP |
| Profit % | (Profit / CP) × 100 |
| Loss % | (Loss / CP) × 100 |
| SP when profit is p% | CP × (100 + p) / 100 |
| SP when loss is l% | CP × (100 − l) / 100 |
| CP from SP and profit p% | SP × 100 / (100 + p) |

> **Profit % and loss % are always calculated on CP**, unless the question clearly says otherwise.

### Marked price and discount

- **Marked Price (MP)** — the price printed on the tag.
- **Discount** is always given on the **MP**: SP = MP − Discount.
- Discount % = (Discount / MP) × 100

A useful chain that links all three:

**CP × (100 + profit%) = MP × (100 − discount%)**

```calc
A shopkeeper marks goods 40% above CP and gives a 10% discount. Profit %?
Let CP = 100 → MP = 140
SP = 140 × 0.9 = 126
Profit = 26%
```

### Shortcut 1 — Assume CP = 100

Turn every percentage into a plain number by taking CP = 100. This works for almost every question that gives no actual prices.

### Shortcut 2 — Successive discounts

Two discounts of a% and b% are **not** a + b. Use the same rule as successive percentage change:

**Single equivalent discount = a + b − (a × b)/100**

```calc
Discounts of 20% and 10% in succession equal a single discount of:
20 + 10 − (20 × 10)/100 = 30 − 2 = 28%
```

### Shortcut 3 — Same SP, one profit and one loss of x%

If two items are sold at the **same selling price**, one at x% profit and the other at x% loss, there is **always a loss** of:

**Loss % = x² / 100**

```calc
Two phones are sold at ₹9,900 each — one at 10% profit, one at 10% loss.
Overall result = loss of 10²/100 = 1%
```

### Shortcut 4 — Dishonest dealer (false weights)

A dealer who claims to sell at cost price but uses a false weight makes a profit of:

**Profit % = Error / (True value − Error) × 100**

In other words: (True weight − False weight) / False weight × 100.

```calc
A dealer uses a 900 g weight in place of 1 kg.
Profit % = 100 / 900 × 100 = 11.11%
```

### Shortcut 5 — CP of x items = SP of y items

If the cost price of **x** items equals the selling price of **y** items:

**Profit % = (x − y) / y × 100** (a negative answer means a loss)

```calc
CP of 12 pens = SP of 10 pens.
Profit % = (12 − 10)/10 × 100 = 20%
```

### Shortcut 6 — Find CP from two outcomes

If selling at SP₁ gives a profit p% and selling at SP₂ gives a loss l%, the difference in selling prices equals the difference in percentages, taken on CP:

**CP = (SP₁ − SP₂) × 100 / (p + l)**

```calc
Selling at ₹540 gives 8% profit; selling at ₹480 gives 4% loss. Find CP.
CP = (540 − 480) × 100 / (8 + 4) = 6000/12 = ₹500
```

### Common traps
- Do not calculate profit % on SP.
- Discount is on MP, while profit is on CP. Keep the two bases apart.
- "Gain" means the same as profit.

=== Ratio and Proportion
---
A **ratio** compares two quantities of the same kind: a : b means "a parts to b parts", which is the same as the fraction a/b. A **proportion** says that two ratios are equal: a : b = c : d.

### Key ideas

- Multiplying or dividing both terms by the same number does **not** change a ratio: 2 : 3 = 4 : 6 = 20 : 30.
- In a : b = c : d, the product of the **extremes** equals the product of the **means**: **a × d = b × c**.
- **Fourth proportional** to a, b, c is x where a : b = c : x, so **x = bc / a**.
- **Mean proportional** between a and b is **√(ab)**.
- **Third proportional** to a and b is **b² / a**.

### Dividing a quantity in a given ratio

To split a total T in the ratio a : b, each share is that part's fraction of the total:

**Share of A = T × a / (a + b)**, **Share of B = T × b / (a + b)**

```calc
Divide ₹1,200 between A and B in the ratio 3 : 5.
Total parts = 8 → 1 part = 1200/8 = 150
A = 3 × 150 = ₹450, B = 5 × 150 = ₹750
```

> **The "1 part" method.** Find what 1 part is worth, then multiply. This is faster and less error-prone than using formulas.

### Combining ratios (A : B and B : C → A : B : C)

Make the common term (B) equal in both ratios.

```calc
A : B = 2 : 3 and B : C = 4 : 5. Find A : B : C.
Make B the same: LCM of 3 and 4 = 12
A : B = 8 : 12 and B : C = 12 : 15
A : B : C = 8 : 12 : 15
```

**Shortcut:** For A : B = a : b and B : C = c : d, the answer is **A : B : C = ac : bc : bd**.

### Ratio when values are added or removed

Write the parts as **ax** and **bx**, then form an equation.

```calc
Two numbers are in the ratio 3 : 4. When 5 is added to each, the ratio becomes 4 : 5. Find the numbers.
(3x + 5) / (4x + 5) = 4/5
15x + 25 = 16x + 20 → x = 5
Numbers are 15 and 20
```

### Shortcut — Check the answer options

Many ratio questions can be solved by testing the options. The correct pair must be in the given ratio **and** satisfy the other condition. This often takes seconds.

### Ages (a common ratio application)

The **difference between two people's ages never changes**. Use this when the ratio changes over time.

```calc
The ages of A and B are in the ratio 5 : 3. After 6 years the ratio is 7 : 5. Find their present ages.
(5x + 6) / (3x + 6) = 7/5
25x + 30 = 21x + 42 → 4x = 12 → x = 3
A = 15 years, B = 9 years
```

### Comparing ratios

To find which of a/b and c/d is bigger, cross-multiply: compare **a × d** with **b × c**.

### Common traps
- A ratio only works when both quantities are in the **same unit**. Convert first (for example, 1 m : 50 cm = 100 : 50 = 2 : 1).
- In A : B = 2 : 3, A is **2/5** of the total, not 2/3.

=== Simple and Compound Interest
---
When you borrow money, the original amount is the **Principal (P)**. The extra you pay for using it is the **Interest**. **Amount (A) = Principal + Interest**.

### Simple Interest (SI)

Interest is calculated only on the original principal, so it is the **same every year**.

**SI = (P × R × T) / 100**

where R = rate per year in %, and T = time in years.

**Amount = P + SI = P × (1 + RT/100)**

```calc
Find the SI on ₹5,000 at 8% per year for 3 years.
SI = 5000 × 8 × 3 / 100 = ₹1,200
Amount = ₹6,200
```

**Shortcuts for SI**
- A sum **doubles** in T years at SI → R = 100 / T. (It doubles in 10 years at 10%.)
- A sum becomes **n times** in T years → R = (n − 1) × 100 / T.
- If time is in months, divide by 12. If it is in days, divide by 365.

### Compound Interest (CI)

Interest is added to the principal at the end of each period, and the next period's interest is charged on that new, larger amount. This is "interest on interest".

**A = P × (1 + R/100)ⁿ** and **CI = A − P**

| Compounding | Rate to use | Number of periods |
|---|---|---|
| Yearly | R | n |
| Half-yearly | R/2 | 2n |
| Quarterly | R/4 | 4n |

```calc
Find the CI on ₹10,000 at 10% per year for 2 years.
A = 10000 × 1.1 × 1.1 = 12,100
CI = ₹2,100
```

### Shortcut 1 — CI as successive percentages

CI for 2 years at R% is just "R% then R%":

**Effective 2-year rate = 2R + R²/100**

```calc
CI for 2 years at 10% = 20 + 1 = 21% of P
On ₹10,000 that is ₹2,100 — no powers needed
```

For 3 years: **3R + 3R²/100 + R³/10000**. At 10%, that is 30 + 3 + 0.1 = **33.1%**.

### Shortcut 2 — Difference between CI and SI

For the same P and R:

- **2 years: CI − SI = P × (R/100)²**
- **3 years: CI − SI = P × (R/100)² × (3 + R/100)**

```calc
The difference between CI and SI on a sum for 2 years at 5% is ₹25. Find the sum.
25 = P × (5/100)² = P/400 → P = ₹10,000
```

### Shortcut 3 — Doubling with CI (Rule of 72)

At R% compound interest, money roughly doubles in **72 / R years**. At 8% it takes about 9 years. This is handy for checking options quickly.

### Shortcut 4 — CI over consecutive years

At CI, each year's amount is **(1 + R/100)** times the previous year's. So if the amount is A₁ after year n and A₂ after year n + 1:

**R = (A₂ − A₁) / A₁ × 100**

```calc
A sum amounts to ₹8,820 in 2 years and ₹9,261 in 3 years at CI. Find the rate.
R = (9261 − 8820)/8820 × 100 = 441/8820 × 100 = 5%
```

### Common traps
- For half-yearly or quarterly compounding, change **both** the rate and the number of periods.
- When a question asks for CI, subtract P from the amount at the end.
- SI grows in a straight line; CI grows faster every year. For 1 year with yearly compounding, CI = SI.

=== Time and Work
---
Time and Work questions ask how long people (or machines, or pipes) take to finish a job, alone or together. The key idea is the **work rate**:

**If A can finish a job in n days, A does 1/n of the job per day.**

### The basic rules

- Rates **add up** when people work together.
- **Work = Rate × Time.**
- If A does a job in a days and B in b days, together they take **ab / (a + b)** days.

```calc
A finishes a job in 10 days, B in 15 days. Together?
Together = 10 × 15 / (10 + 15) = 150/25 = 6 days
```

### Shortcut 1 — The LCM method (fastest in exams)

Assume the **total work = LCM** of the given days. Then every rate becomes a whole number.

```calc
A: 10 days, B: 15 days, C: 30 days. All together?
Total work = LCM(10, 15, 30) = 30 units
A = 3 units/day, B = 2 units/day, C = 1 unit/day
Together = 6 units/day → 30/6 = 5 days
```

```calc
A and B together take 12 days. A alone takes 20 days. B alone?
Total = LCM(12, 20) = 60 units
A + B = 5 units/day, A = 3 units/day → B = 2 units/day
B alone = 60/2 = 30 days
```

### Shortcut 2 — Someone leaves or joins partway

Work out the units done in each phase and subtract from the total.

```calc
A (20 days) and B (30 days) work together for 6 days, then A leaves. How long does B take to finish?
Total = 60 units; A = 3/day, B = 2/day
6 days together = 6 × 5 = 30 units → 30 left
B alone: 30/2 = 15 more days
```

### Shortcut 3 — Efficiency

If A is **twice as efficient** as B, A takes **half the time**. Efficiency and time are inversely proportional.

```calc
A is 50% more efficient than B. B takes 30 days. A?
Efficiency ratio A : B = 3 : 2 → time ratio = 2 : 3
A = 30 × 2/3 = 20 days
```

### Shortcut 4 — Man-days formula (M₁D₁H₁/W₁ = M₂D₂H₂/W₂)

When the number of workers, days, hours per day or amount of work changes:

**(M₁ × D₁ × H₁) / W₁ = (M₂ × D₂ × H₂) / W₂**

M = men, D = days, H = hours per day, W = work done.

```calc
12 men finish a job in 10 days. How many days will 15 men take?
12 × 10 = 15 × D₂ → D₂ = 8 days
```

### Pipes and cisterns

The same idea applies to pipes. An **inlet** pipe does positive work and an **outlet** (leak) does negative work.

```calc
Pipe A fills a tank in 6 h, pipe B fills it in 8 h, and leak C empties it in 12 h. All open?
Tank = LCM(6, 8, 12) = 24 units
A = +4, B = +3, C = −2 → net 5 units/h
Time = 24/5 = 4.8 h = 4 h 48 min
```

### Wages

Wages are shared in the ratio of **work done**. When people work for the same number of days, that is the ratio of their efficiencies.

### Common traps
- Do not add the days. Add the **rates** (the work per day).
- Convert fractions of an hour to minutes carefully: 0.8 h = 48 min, not 80 min.

=== Time, Speed and Distance
---
Everything in this topic comes from one formula:

**Distance = Speed × Time**, so Speed = Distance / Time and Time = Distance / Speed.

### Unit conversion

- **km/h → m/s: multiply by 5/18**
- **m/s → km/h: multiply by 18/5**

```calc
72 km/h in m/s = 72 × 5/18 = 20 m/s
15 m/s in km/h = 15 × 18/5 = 54 km/h
```

### Shortcut 1 — Average speed is NOT the average of the speeds

If equal **distances** are covered at speeds x and y:

**Average speed = 2xy / (x + y)**

```calc
A car goes from A to B at 40 km/h and returns at 60 km/h. Average speed?
2 × 40 × 60 / (40 + 60) = 4800/100 = 48 km/h (not 50)
```

If equal **times** are spent at speeds x and y, the average speed is simply (x + y)/2.

In general: **Average speed = Total distance / Total time.**

### Shortcut 2 — Relative speed

- Objects moving in **opposite directions**: relative speed = **a + b**.
- Objects moving in the **same direction**: relative speed = **a − b**.

### Trains

- A train passing a **pole or a person** covers its **own length**.
- A train passing a **platform, bridge or another train** covers **its own length + the other length**.

```calc
A 150 m train runs at 54 km/h. How long does it take to cross a 250 m platform?
Speed = 54 × 5/18 = 15 m/s
Distance = 150 + 250 = 400 m
Time = 400/15 = 26.67 s
```

```calc
Two trains of 120 m and 180 m run in opposite directions at 40 km/h and 50 km/h. Time to cross each other?
Relative speed = 90 km/h = 25 m/s
Distance = 120 + 180 = 300 m → Time = 12 s
```

### Boats and streams

Let b = speed of the boat in still water, and s = speed of the stream.

- **Downstream** (with the current): **b + s**
- **Upstream** (against the current): **b − s**
- **b = (Down + Up) / 2** and **s = (Down − Up) / 2**

```calc
A boat goes 12 km/h downstream and 8 km/h upstream.
Boat speed = (12 + 8)/2 = 10 km/h, stream speed = (12 − 8)/2 = 2 km/h
```

### Shortcut 3 — Ratio thinking

- For the **same distance**, speed and time are **inversely proportional**. If the speed ratio is 3 : 4, the time ratio is 4 : 3.
- For the **same time**, distance is proportional to speed.

```calc
Walking at 3/4 of his usual speed, a man is 20 min late. Usual time?
Speed ratio 3 : 4 → time ratio 4 : 3
Extra 1 part = 20 min → usual time = 3 parts = 60 min
```

### Shortcut 4 — Late and early problems

If a person is **t₁ late** at speed S₁ and **t₂ early** at speed S₂, then:

**Distance = S₁ × S₂ × (t₁ + t₂) / (S₂ − S₁)**, with times in hours.

### Meeting point

Two people start towards each other from a distance D apart. They meet after **D / (a + b)** hours.

### Common traps
- Do not mix units. Convert everything to m/s or to km/h before calculating.
- For trains, remember to add the train's own length.
- Use Total distance / Total time for average speed, never the plain average of the speeds over equal distances.
